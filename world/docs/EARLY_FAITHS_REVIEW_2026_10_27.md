# Early Jewish, Christian, Buddhist and Sikh history · 27 October 2026

This continuation adds 13 original bilingual histories and four connected reading paths. The [baseline](EARLY_FAITHS_BASELINE_2026_10_27.json) pins the 1,896-entry published corpus, its narrative inputs and historical-map manifest. Focus intervals are inclusive editorial windows; they do not define a religion's lifespan. No new territorial geometry, journey line or claim of exclusive religious occupancy has been added. Points mark a selected site or a broad context. These are source-backed editorial syntheses, not specialist or community acceptance.

## Jewish communities and rabbinic traditions

- `jewish-communities-second-temple` uses **539 BCE–70 CE** as a comparison from Cyrus's conquest of Babylon to the destruction of the Second Temple. The first year is not the Temple's completion or the beginning of Jewish history. The directly inspected [My Jewish Learning overview](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/539-bce-to-632-ce-the-story/) describes earlier Babylonian exile, people who remained in the land, later returns and communities in Egypt and Asia Minor. It is a broad teaching synthesis; its simplifying claim about a 315 CE Christian empire is not adopted. The [Met's late-antique Jewish art essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/jewish-art-in-late-antiquity-and-early-byzantium) supplies selected material evidence, not a total population history.
- `jerusalem-second-temple-destruction-70` dates the Roman siege and Temple destruction to **70 CE**. This is neither a single start of the Jewish diaspora nor the end of Jewish life in the land or elsewhere. In [Rabba Alieza Salzberg Yizhar's essay](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/judaism-after-the-temple/), the later Yohanan ben Zakkai coffin-escape story is explicitly treated as historically questionable, rather than a transparent contemporaneous report.
- `rabbinic-judaism-after-70` uses **70–300 CE** as a focus on selected later generations, not a claim that every earlier practice was immediately replaced or that rabbinic development ended in 300. The cited essay discusses Torah study, law and study in the land and Babylonia. Its community perspective and the limits of later narrative are stated in the entry.

## Early Christianity

- `early-christianity-jewish-context` places Jesus and the first followers within diverse first-century Judaism; it does **not** assign an exact birth or death year. [Markus Tiwald's University of Arizona-hosted essay](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/beginnings-christianity-integral-part-early-judaism) argues that Christian–Jewish differentiation was extended and regionally uneven. That is an attributed scholarly interpretation, not a universally agreed date or a claim that Judaism ended.
- `paul-corinth-letters-50s` uses **50–55 CE** as an approximate context rather than a complete life or itinerary. The directly inspected [PBS interviews with Wayne Meeks and L. Michael White](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/pages/frontline/shows/religion/first/missions.html) discuss Paul’s letters, Jewish and Gentile participants, shared meals and observance; White describes approximately the 50s while noting difficult dates and differences between Paul and Acts. PBS identifies the 1998 page as legacy material that may be outdated. No death date or invented sequence of travel legs has been added.
- `christian-jewish-communities-late-antiquity` compares **100–400 CE** without claiming uniform practice or a universal separation year. Tiwald's reading of later writers is an interpretation; the Met describes a third-century Dura-Europos synagogue near a Christian meeting house as evidence for particular communities, not all interactions everywhere.
- `milan-toleration-313` and `council-of-nicaea-325` separate two events. The [Internet History Sourcebooks translation, hosted by Fordham](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/edict-milan.asp), reproduces Lactantius chapter 48 on Constantine, Licinius, Mediolanum, freedom of worship and the return of Christian communal property; it is a translation, not an independently inspected original inscription. It also distinguishes an earlier **311** edict. The [Carnuntum museum account](https://www.carnuntum.at/en/magazine/politics-and-the-world-of-faith-in-late-antiquity-1700-years-of-the-council-of-nicaea/1314) dates Nicaea to **325** and describes a creed and disputes involving Arius. Neither 313 nor 325 is Christianity's origin; the 313 policy is not treated as establishment of a state religion or proof that all persecution stopped.

## Buddhism

- `early-buddhist-teachings-communities` follows the directly inspected [Met essay by Vidya Dehejia](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/buddhism-and-buddhist-art), which places the Buddha broadly in the **fifth–fourth centuries BCE** and discusses Buddhist teaching, monasteries, stupas and pilgrims. **500–200 BCE** is a reading window, not an exact life or founding date. [Translated Ashokan inscriptions](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/india/ashoka-edicts.asp), inspected in an earlier review, are royal statements and not a contemporaneous biography of the Buddha.
- `lumbini-ashoka-pillar-249-bce` follows [UNESCO's site account](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/666/) in assigning the pillar to approximately **249 BCE**. Its separate traditional **623 BCE** Buddha birth date conflicts with the Met essay's broad chronology and is not adopted as settled fact. The inscription is a later object; it cannot independently establish an exact birth year. UNESCO also describes later monastic remains.
- `buddhist-art-mathura-gandhara` compares the Met's examples of first-century BCE stone railings and gateways and first-century CE Buddha images at Gandhara and Mathura. The **100 BCE–200 CE** window is a selected comparison, not proof of a universal first image, identical practice or the end of Buddhist art.

## Sikh traditions

- `sikh-gurus-punjab-1469-1708` and `khalsa-anandpur-1699` use the directly inspected [Sikh Coalition history](https://www.sikhcoalition.org/about-sikhs/history/) for **1469** Guru Nanak, the ten Gurus, the **1699** Vaisakhi gathering at Anandpur and **1708** death of the tenth human Guru. The Sikh community account identifies the Guru Granth Sahib and Guru Khalsa Panth as continuing authorities. The [Sikhs.org Sikh Heritage Gallery virtual tour](https://www.sikhs.org/smithsonian/gurus.html) corroborates the 1469–1539 and 1666–1708 life ranges; it is hosted by Sikhs.org, not the Smithsonian website, and does not independently date the 1699 gathering. These are community interpretations; further Sikh scholarship and community review can deepen them.
- The early Sikh period, Khalsa gathering and **1799–1849** Sikh kingdom are separate subjects. The ending of the selected Guru-era or kingdom focus does not mark the end of Sikhism. No new personal biography was created: the directly inspected sources do not provide three securely dated and located life milestones sufficient for the project's biography standard. A [Sikh Museum Initiative blog](https://www.sikhmuseum.org.uk/fascinating-facts-about-guru-gobind-singh-the-tenth-sikh-guru/) supplies additional place context but is adapted from a news article and contains an age inconsistency with the 1666 birth year; the disputed chronology was not adopted.
